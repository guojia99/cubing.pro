# Professional Cooking Terms

Cooking is like learning to program: first you need to learn the terminology, just as you learn basic syntax. Combining these techniques is how you complete a dish.

【Chao (stir-frying)】Historically written with an alternate character also pronounced chao, this is one of the most basic cooking methods today: cut food into small pieces, put it in an iron wok containing very hot oil, toss rapidly until cooked, then add seasonings.

【Qiang (blanching and dressing with hot spices)】Cut the food, briefly blanch or immerse it in boiling water or hot oil, then toss it with dried chiles and Sichuan pepper oil flashed in a hot wok.

【Chui (cooking by steaming or boiling)】Cooking food by steaming, boiling, or similar methods. This term appears most often in the names of Chaozhou dishes.

【Zhu (boiling)】One of the simplest cooking methods: cook food in a wok or pot with an appropriate amount of boiling water or broth and seasonings.

【Jian (pan-frying)】Heat an iron wok, add a little cooking oil, then spread the food flat against the wok. Use low heat and hot oil to cook it through and turn the surface golden brown.

【Bao (flash-cooking over high heat)】Use a hot wok and hot oil, splash in an appropriate amount of prepared sauce or broth, and quickly cook small pieces of food while imparting aroma.

【Zha (deep-frying)】Historically written with an alternate character also pronounced zha, this is one of the most common cooking methods: immerse food in a large amount of hot oil and cook until crisp.

【Xia (boiling until tender)】Historically written with the character zha, this preparation method uses a large amount of boiling water over a stove to soften and cook foods with a tougher texture.

【Gun (rolling-boil treatment)】Use the movement of a large amount of vigorously boiling water to draw unpleasant flavors out of food.

【Cuan (cooking small pieces in boiling water)】A northern Chinese cooking term, historically written as chuan. It is similar to the Cantonese technique lu: shape food into balls or cut it into slices, cook it in boiling water, lift it into a bowl, and add boiling broth.

【Zhuo (quick blanching)】Called chao in northern Chinese writing. Cut food into thin slices or similar pieces, quickly cook it in boiling water, then eat it dipped in sauce.

【Da (simmering vegetables until tender and green)】Put vegetables into boiling water containing alkaline water or cooking oil, then simmer over low heat until cooked through, leaving them soft and bright green.

【Shuan (swishing in hot broth)】A northern Chinese cooking term: cook thinly sliced food in spicy broth, then eat it dipped in sauce.

【Kuo (covered cooking with aromatics)】Historically written as yu. Put food directly into a wok or clay pot, add generous amounts of ginger, scallions, and other aromatics, cover, and use the abundant aromatics to perfume the food as it cooks through.

【Ju (sealed cooking in hot salt)】Seal food in foil or yukou paper, a wrapping paper, and cook it in searing-hot coarse salt or a similar medium under sealed conditions.

【Men (covered braising)】A northern Chinese cooking method: put tough-textured food into a wok, add an appropriate amount of broth, cover, and cook over low heat until tender and done.

【Wen (gentle braising)】Similar to the northern Chinese technique shao, hence the saying "wen in the south, shao in the north." Put tough-textured food into a wok, add an appropriate amount of broth, and cook over low heat until tender and done.

【Hui (stewing mixed ingredients)】Cook various meats and vegetables together in an appropriate amount of broth.

【Zheng (steaming)】Cook food using the heat of water vapor.

【Dun (double-steaming or stewing)】Add water or broth to food in a lidded container, cover it, then use the heat of steam to cook the food and produce soup. In northern Chinese cuisines, this means cooking food in a large amount of broth over low heat until tender and done.

【Kou (steaming in a bowl and inverting)】Season and precook the food, arrange it neatly in a molding bowl, and steam it over water. Invert the main ingredients onto a plate, then pour over a glossy, clear sauce thickened from the original cooking juices.

【Bao (slow simmering for soup)】Put food into a large amount of water and cook it over low heat on the stove to cook the ingredients and produce soup.

【Ao (long simmering and reducing)】Use low heat over a long period to draw savory flavor from meat into broth while concentrating the broth.

【Kao (slowly infusing flavor)】Use strongly flavored ingredients and fresh stock over low heat for an extended time to impart savory flavor to another, bland main ingredient. This can be either a preparation step or a cooking method.

【Wei (simmering with aromatics)】Originally meant cooking food buried in charcoal ashes. Today, it refers to preparing food with ginger, scallions, and broth to add flavor and remove unwanted odors. In northern Chinese cuisines, it also means cooking food with broth in a sealed earthenware jar over low heat.

【Wu (cooking in a sealed outer coating)】Takes the place of the old meaning of wei: marinate food, wrap it in lotus leaves or similar materials, seal it in wet mud or dough, and cook it in a charcoal fire.

【Hong (baking)】Season or prepare dim sum or other food, then cook it in an oven.

【Bian (stir-frying to remove moisture)】Also called bi; formerly miswritten as bian, meaning "whip," or as the separate components huo, meaning "fire," and bian. Similar to ran, or dry-heating, this means continually stir-frying food in a hot wok to dry out some moisture and remove unpleasant flavors. It can also be a cooking method that concentrates savory flavor before serving.

【Liu (coating with a thickened sauce)】A northern Chinese cooking term, similar to the Cantonese da qian, or sauce-thickening: thicken a sweet-and-sour sauce with starch to give crisp-fried food a smooth, tender, appetizing coating.

【Geng (making thick soup)】One of the oldest cooking methods: cook diced food in boiling broth, then gradually add a starch slurry to thicken the broth to a paste-like consistency.

【Zan (splashing with hot oil or wine)】Formerly written as jian or zan, among other forms. There are two types: zan you, splashing with oil, and zan jiu, splashing with wine. The former means pouring boiling-hot oil over steamed food to remove fishy odors and give it a silkier texture. The latter means splashing Shaoxing wine onto food as it cooks to give it more wok hei, the characteristic aroma of wok cooking.

【Tang (scalding)】A preparation method that uses boiling water to tighten the outer surface of meat. In northern China, it commonly means cooking sliced or cut-up ingredients in boiling broth or spicy broth.

【Shao (roasting over fire or braising)】The ancient technique zhi, or fire-roasting. In Cantonese cuisine, this means cooking food over charcoal or an open flame. In present-day northern Chinese cuisines, it means cooking food over low heat while slightly reducing the sauce.

【Kao (roasting over an open flame)】In northern Chinese cuisines, this replaces the old meaning of shao, hence the saying "shao in the south, kao in the north." It means cooking food over an open flame.

【Lu (cooking in a seasoned master sauce)】Cook food or infuse it with flavor in a master sauce made with light soy sauce, spices, and medicinal herbs.

【Jiang (cooking or flavoring with sauce)】Use a large amount of sauce or light soy sauce to flavor or cook food.

【Jin (poaching by immersion)】Cook food for a set period in a large amount of boiling water or broth, with the heat kept at the "chrysanthemum center" stage, where bubbles cluster in the center. Similar to the northern Chinese tun technique: blanch food until cooked, then ladle over enough broth to cover it before serving.

【Feng (air-drying)】Hang cured food in a ventilated place at any time of year and let it dry naturally in the shade or wind.

【La (winter air-drying)】Around the twelfth month of the lunar calendar, hang cured food in a ventilated place and let it dry naturally in the shade or wind.

【Yan (smoking)】Burn tea flavoring or aromatic spices and medicinal herbs in an enclosed space to give food their fragrant smoky flavor.

【Xun (dry smoking or moist scenting)】Also written as xun in the past. There are two types: gan xun, or dry smoking, is similar to yan; shi xun, or moist scenting, uses fresh flowers, Shaoxing wine, or similar ingredients to impart aroma to food.

【Zao (flavoring or cooking in wine lees)】Put food into wine lees to flavor or cook it.

【Zui (flavoring or cooking with distilled liquor)】Use a large amount of distilled liquor to flavor or cook food.

【Zeng (steaming in an earthenware bowl)】The ancient form of zheng, or steaming: chop food into pieces, season it, put it in an earthenware bowl, and cook it with strong steam.

【Dong (setting into an aspic)】Also called shuijing, meaning "crystal." Add agar, pork skin, or similar ingredients to food that has been cooked until very soft, cook it into a thick soup, then refrigerate until cold and set before eating.

【Fei shui (brief blanching)】Briefly dip food in boiling water until partly cooked, then remove it quickly to provide a good foundation for subsequent cooking.

【Bing jin (ice-water soaking)】Cut food into fine strips and quickly immerse it in ice water to give it a crisp texture. This preparation and cooking technique originated in Japan.

【Bo si (spun-sugar coating)】Coat food in a starch mixture and deep-fry it, then toss it in melted sugar syrup so that fine sugar threads form when a piece is picked up.

【Gua shuang (sugar-frosting)】Deep-fry food, then toss it in melted sugar syrup and separate the pieces, or sprinkle it directly with powdered sugar.

【Jiao yan (salt-and-pepper tossing)】Deep-fry food until cooked and dry on the surface, then stir-fry and toss it with a premixed seasoning of crushed pepper and fine salt.

【You pao (quick cooking in hot oil)】Quickly cook food in a large amount of hot oil.

【Zou you (passing through hot oil)】Also called tuo you, zou you, or pao you, meaning dragging, passing, or running through oil. Quickly pass prepared ingredients through very hot oil to prepare them for subsequent cooking.

【Huo yan (flame cooking)】Put very fresh, live seafood in a glass vessel and cook it with the heat produced by igniting high-proof baijiu, a Chinese distilled spirit.

【Ze ze (sizzling clay-pot cooking)】Put food, ginger, scallions, and other ingredients in an extremely hot clay pot so the food makes a "ze ze" sizzling sound and releases its aroma.

【Chuan shao (skewer cooking)】Slice and marinate meat, thread it onto bamboo skewers, and cook it by immersing it in hot oil before eating. Alternatively, slice meat, thread it onto metal skewers, roast it over charcoal, and sprinkle it with cumin and other seasonings.

【Tie ban (sizzling-plate cooking)】Originally a Western cooking method: after passing food through hot oil, put it onto an extremely hot iron plate with sauce and aromatics, mainly onions, to cook it through and release its aroma.

【Sang na (sauna cooking)】Also called shi peng, or stone cooking. Pass food through oil, place it on searing-hot stones (usually Yuhua, or rain-flower, pebbles), then splash on prepared sauce or broth. The steam cooks the food or releases its aroma.

【Jian feng (pan-frying and sealing with sauce)】Also called jian peng in northern China; most commonly suited to fish. Marinate the fish with seasonings, pan-fry it thoroughly in hot oil over low heat, then cover it with a thickened sauce containing aromatics so the flavors penetrate.

【Wo tie (frying against a layer of fat)】A form of half pan-frying, half deep-frying: coat marinated meat with wo tie batter, press it onto a layer of fatty meat, and use a very hot wok with relatively cool oil to make one side crisp and the other soft and silky.

【Wo ta (batter-frying and simmering)】Coat marinated food in egg-and-starch batter, cook it by pan-frying first and then deep-frying, then add seasoned fresh stock and cook it through again.

【Ruan jian (soft pan-frying)】A form of half pan-frying, half deep-frying: coat marinated meat with egg-and-starch batter, cook it by pan-frying first and then deep-frying, then cut it into pieces and pour sauce over it.

【Dan jian (egg pan-frying)】Precook meat by brief blanching or quick cooking in hot oil, mix it into seasoned beaten egg, then pan-fry the meat-and-egg mixture over low heat until golden brown on both sides.

【Ji lie (cutlet-style frying)】A phonetic rendering of the English word CUTLET: coat food in beaten egg, cover it with breadcrumbs, then deep-fry it in hot oil. This method originated in Western cooking.

【Su zha (crisp-frying)】Marinate food with seasonings, coat it first in a wet batter and then in dry starch, deep-fry it in hot oil, then lift it into sauce.

【Huo guo (hot pot)】Also called shuan guo, or swishing pot, and da bian lu in Guangdong. Slice fresh meat thinly, or beat it into balls, rounded portions, filling, and so on, then bring it to the diners with vegetables and other ingredients. Diners cook the food themselves in boiling water or broth.

【Qi guo (steam-pot cooking)】Marinate meat, place it with medicinal herbs in a specially made qi guo, or steam pot, containing boiling seasoned broth, and simmer gently before eating.

【Liang ban (cold tossing)】Cut cooked foods, vegetables, or fruit as needed, add seasonings, and toss until evenly mixed.

【Yu sheng (raw fish preparation)】Drain the blood from very fresh, live aquatic ingredients, cut them into thin slices, and toss with shredded ginger, shredded scallions, crisp fried wafers, shredded lemon, and similar ingredients. Eat dipped in light soy sauce.

【Ci shen (sashimi)】Originally a Japanese preparation referring to raw meat slices. As adopted into Chinese cuisine, it means scaling and cleaning the blood from fresh, live freshwater or marine ingredients, slicing them thinly, adding drops of lemon juice, and eating them dipped in Japanese wasabi.

【Zhu tong (bamboo-tube cooking)】Historically called zeng. Use a bamboo tube as the cooking vessel and cook the food by roasting, cooking over fire, steaming, double-steaming, or similar methods.

【Mi zhi (sweet-syrup cooking)】Dissolve white sugar, honey, maltose, or similar ingredients into a thick syrup, add the prepared ingredients, and use long simmering, steaming, or similar methods to make them soft and sticky, with sweetness and syrup absorbed throughout.

【Chao shui (blanching)】Also called chu shui, or water treatment. Put ingredients into a pot of boiling or cold water to partially cook them as a preliminary step.

【Guo you (precooking in oil)】Use oil as the heat-transfer medium to partially cook ingredients. Passing small ingredients through warm oil is also called hua you, or oil-velveting; passing large ingredients through very hot oil is also called zou you, or passing through oil.

【Gua hu (battering)】Evenly coat ingredients with a layer of batter before cooking.

【Shang jiang (velveting)】Mix ingredients with starch, egg, salt, and similar ingredients to coat their surfaces in a thin layer of slurry.

【Shang jin (mixing to develop springiness)】Add fine salt, water, starch, and other supplementary ingredients to finely minced or pureed animal ingredients, then stir repeatedly until the mixture is glossy and tender and neither sinks nor falls apart in water.

【Gou qian (thickening with starch)】Add a starch-and-water solution to the pan during cooking to give the dish's liquid a certain thickness. Also called zhuo ni, zhuo qian, or long qian, alternate terms for thickening a sauce.

【Wen you (warm oil)】Commonly called the three- to four-tenths stage, generally 70℃~100℃.

【Re you (hot oil)】Commonly called the five- to six-tenths stage, generally 110℃~170℃.

【Wang you (very hot oil)】Commonly called the seven- to eight-tenths stage, generally 180℃~220℃.

【Hua guo (oiling the wok)】Heat the wok, drizzle in a little oil, swirl it over the entire surface, then pour the oil out.

【Qiang guo (blooming aromatics in the wok)】Also called zha guo, or frying the wok. Put ginger, scallions, minced chiles, or other aromatic seasonings into the hot starting oil in a wok, stir-fry until fragrant, then promptly add the main ingredients.

【Gao tang (clarified stock)】Also called qing tang, shang tang, or ding tang, meaning clear stock, superior stock, or top stock. Make a basic stock by simmering pork bones, chicken bones, duck carcasses, meat scraps, and similar ingredients. Treat it with unsalted minced chicken or meat mixed into a thin paste, producing a stock that is clear as water, rich, and savory.

【Nai tang (milky stock)】Also called bai tang, or white stock. A milky-white soup made by slowly simmering ingredients in water.
