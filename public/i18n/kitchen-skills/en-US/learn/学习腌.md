# Marinating (Meat)

## Note

The marinating described here is a preparation step before cooking ingredients, not a method for making finished products such as salt-cured pork or cured sausages.

## Marinating

Marinating meat before cooking is a common way to season it in advance. Usually, raw meat is marinated. Cut the meat to whatever size the dish requires.

  For example, for popcorn chicken, cut chicken breast into small, dice-sized pieces before putting it in a bowl to marinate.
  
  For example, for whole roast lamb, a leg of lamb, half a lamb carcass, or a whole lamb carcass does not need to be cut up; instead, rub plenty of seasonings over the surface to marinate it and let the flavors penetrate.

Depending on the dish, any kind of seasoning or supplementary ingredient can be used in a marinade. Sometimes, supplementary ingredients also need to be prepared in advance to achieve different flavors.

## Marinating Basics

This section describes marinating for a typical level of seasoning.

- Generally, the greater the quantity of meat (such as marinating 5kg of chicken wings at once), the larger the individual piece (such as a whole leg of lamb), or the stronger the desired flavor, the more seasonings and supplementary ingredients you need.
- Generally, the longer you plan to marinate, the fewer seasonings and supplementary ingredients you need.
- When marinating, coat all surfaces evenly with the marinade. For sliced or shredded meat, use your hands to toss and mix it as evenly as possible. For a whole leg of lamb, spread the marinade evenly over the surface with your hands or a brush.
- Meat for stir-frying or deep-frying generally needs to be marinated in advance. Stir-fried meat should stay tender and juicy, so it is often cooked briefly over high heat. When a short cooking time does not allow enough flavor to penetrate, marinating in advance makes up for the lack of seasoning.

## Marinating Techniques

- Fine shreds or thin slices of meat: Handle these as gently as possible because they are delicate. Shape your fingers like the claw in a claw machine and gently toss the meat with the marinade. Then stir gently in one direction until evenly mixed.
- Shreds, slices, or chunks of meat: Use the same technique as above, but you can apply a little more force.
- Pieces the size of chicken drumsticks or wings: First make a few decorative slashes in the ingredients. For drumettes and drumsticks, you can pierce holes all the way through with a knife. Mix the marinade in a bowl first, then add the ingredients and coat them evenly.
- Pieces the size of a leg of lamb: Generally, you can pierce holes all the way through the thicker parts of the meat. Mix the marinade, then spread it evenly over the surface.

## Marinating Containers and Times

- Choose any container that can hold the ingredients and marinade, including a bowl, plate, or tray. This is open-container marinating, which is generally brief, commonly 0.5-2 hours. A leg of lamb for roasting can also be marinated this way, but it takes longer.
- You can use a food storage bag large enough to seal around the ingredients. This is sealed marinating, which generally takes a long time, such as overnight, or is used for ingredients that do not readily absorb flavor, such as ribs. Common times range from 4 hours to overnight. Use slightly less seasoning to keep the finished dish from being too strongly flavored or too salty.

## Common Marinade Ingredients

- Light soy sauce: Provides a salty base with a soy sauce aroma. Can be used for almost any meat.
- Dark soy sauce: Not particularly salty, but colors ingredients easily. Used to establish the base color and add aroma. Generally, do not use large amounts, to avoid a raw bean flavor. Can be used for almost any red meat (in small amounts); more can be used for ingredients such as pork liver.
- Salt: Adds saltiness without a soy sauce aroma after stir-frying. Can be used for all meats.
- White (granulated) sugar: Adds sweetness in larger amounts, or a tender, juicy texture in smaller amounts. Can be used for all poultry and livestock meats, but is not commonly used for fish or seafood.
- Brown sugar: Adds sweetness and the distinctive taste of brown sugar, with a slightly stronger flavor than white sugar. Can be used for almost any meat (generally darker meat or dishes with a darker finished color).
- Oyster sauce: Adds savory, salty, and sweet flavors. Generally used for red meat.
- White vinegar/rice vinegar: Adds sourness. Used less often.
- Aged black vinegar/aromatic black vinegar: Adds sourness as well as aroma and color. Aromatic black vinegar is particularly suitable for dark-colored fish (especially grilled or roasted fish).
- Cooking wine: Reduces fishy or gamey odors and adds aroma. Can be used for almost any meat. However, keep the following in mind:
  - Cooking wine has a strong flavor of its own and can easily mask the ingredients' natural aroma. For ingredients without a strong fishy or gamey smell, consider omitting it. Examples include beef, fish, and chicken.
  - For chicken, you can substitute baijiu, a Chinese distilled spirit, for cooking wine.
  - For beef, you can substitute ginger-scallion water for cooking wine.
  - For white-fleshed fish, simply cleaning away the traces of blood and membranes removes most of the fishy smell, so omitting cooking wine is recommended.
- Huangjiu (Chinese yellow rice wine): Reduces fishy or gamey odors and adds aroma more effectively than cooking wine, with a more complex aroma. Generally used for white meat. It can also be used for red meat, but its effect is comparable to cooking wine.
- Five-spice powder/thirteen-spice powder: Adds aroma to meat and is the simplest type of spice blend. Five-spice powder simply adds aroma, while thirteen-spice powder has a more distinctive, recognizable aroma. Use these spices sparingly when marinating. Can be used for almost any meat, but is not commonly used for fish or seafood.
- Ground chiles: There are many types of ground chiles. Aside from the variety of chile, they can be classified by grind size into chile powder/ground chile, chile flakes, and so on. In addition to adding heat to meat, they add color to the finished dish. Avoid using too much chile in marinated dishes. Can be used for almost any meat that needs a spicy base flavor, but keep the cooking time under control to prevent the chiles from turning bitter or darkening.
- Ground cumin and ground fennel: Generally, use ground spices rather than whole seeds in marinades so the flavors penetrate the meat more easily. Can be used for almost any red meat and chicken.
- Pepper powders: Add pungent, hot, aromatic, and sharp flavors to meat. Use in moderation so they do not overwhelm other flavors.
  - Ground black pepper: Pungent and hot. Can be used for almost any red meat.
  - Ground white pepper: Pungent and aromatic. Slightly milder than black pepper, with more emphasis on aroma. Can be used for almost any meat.
  - Ground Sichuan pepper: Pungent and sharp, with Sichuan pepper's distinctive, recognizable aroma. Can be used for almost any meat.
- Doubanjiang (fermented bean paste): Adds a fermented bean aroma, saltiness, and heat to meat. Can be used for almost any red meat.
- Scallions, ginger, and garlic: Scallions and ginger reduce fishy, gamey, and other unwanted odors and add aroma; garlic adds a pungent aroma. Cut scallions into segments or slices as needed; ginger is generally sliced and sometimes needs to be peeled; garlic can be sliced or minced. If you do not want scallions and ginger to appear in the finished dish, or want a milder flavor, put pieces of scallion and ginger in a bowl with a very small amount of water and squeeze out their juices, then use the scallion-ginger water to marinate the meat. Garlic is generally not added directly. Can be used for all meats.
- Hoisin sauce, shrimp paste, etc.: Add savory flavor and saltiness to meat. Hoisin sauce tends to be sweet, while shrimp paste has a stronger flavor. Can be used for almost any meat, but their uses are relatively limited.
- Douchi (fermented black beans): Adds fermented bean aroma and saltiness to meat. Can be used for almost any red meat, but is not used very often.
- Starch: This is what the Chinese term shengfen means. Starch is an important marinade ingredient for velveting. The thicker the coating, or the silkier and more tender the desired texture, the more starch is needed. Can be used for almost any meat. `Starch can be used as a simple coating for deep-frying (generally with flour or other ingredients added as needed); in this case, it is generally not added during marinating.`
  - Cornstarch and potato starch: Generally have the greatest binding power.
  - Sweet potato starch: Has slightly less binding power.
- Oil: Adding an appropriate amount of oil as a sealing layer during marinating can lock in moisture and flavor. When marinating in an open container, such as a bowl, for a relatively long time, an oil seal can go a long way toward keeping the meat from becoming dry or tough. Can be used for almost any meat. When stir-frying meat that has been sealed with oil, slightly reduce the oil added to the pan; for deep-frying, no adjustment is needed.

## A Few Broadly Useful Marinade Formulas

- Beef: Marinate with an appropriate amount of light soy sauce, a little cooking wine, and a little white granulated sugar. Depending on the desired flavor, add salt (for extra saltiness), oyster sauce and a tiny amount of hoisin sauce (for beef in oyster sauce), or five-spice powder/thirteen-spice powder (for stir-fried beef with onions). Use scallions and ginger cautiously.
- Chicken (including chicken breast and wings): Marinate with an appropriate amount of light soy sauce, a smaller amount of white granulated sugar, and a little cooking wine. Depending on the desired flavor, add salt (for extra saltiness), five-spice powder/thirteen-spice powder (for popcorn chicken), or a tiny amount of dark soy sauce (for pan-fried wing flats).
- White-fleshed fish: Marinate with an appropriate amount of salt and a little cooking wine/huangjiu. Depending on the desired flavor, add hoisin sauce/seafood soy sauce/seasoned soy sauce for steamed fish (for pan-fried hairtail), or scallions and ginger (or their infused water) (for roasted skin-on fish).
- Red-fleshed fish: Marinate with an appropriate amount of light soy sauce and a little cooking wine. Depending on the desired flavor, add seafood soy sauce/a little seasoned soy sauce for steamed fish (for pan-fried salmon), or brown sugar (for Nordic-style aromatic roasted salmon).
- Pork liver: Marinate with appropriate amounts of light soy sauce and cooking wine. Depending on the desired flavor, add starch and an appropriate amount of dark soy sauce (for silky stir-fried pork liver), a little sugar, etc.

## Practical Dish Examples

- Stir-fried beef with onions: Using a single serving of 150g of beef as an example. Slice the beef; the finished dish should be silky and tender, and is stir-fried.
  - Light soy sauce: 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine: 5ml (about 1 tablespoon)
  - White granulated sugar: 2.5-10g (about 1-4 teaspoons, depending on your preferred sweetness)
  - Ground cumin: 5g (about 2 teaspoons)
  - Starch: 10-15g (about 1 small handful)
  - Oil: 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - (Optional) Thirteen-spice powder: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Ground black pepper: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)

- Beef in oyster sauce: Using a single serving of 150g of beef as an example. Slice the beef; the finished dish should be silky and tender, with a substantial starch coating. This dish leans sweet and is stir-fried.
  - Light soy sauce: 5ml (about 1 tablespoon)
  - Cooking wine: 5ml (about 1 tablespoon)
  - Oyster sauce: 10-20ml (about 2-4 tablespoons, depending on your preferred saltiness; oyster sauce is quite salty)
  - White granulated sugar: 5-15g (about 2-6 teaspoons, depending on your preferred sweetness)
  - Starch: 25-35g (about 1 large handful)
  - Oil: 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)

- Five-spice crispy salted chicken: Using a single serving of 150g of chicken breast as an example. Cut the chicken into dice-shaped pieces; it is deep-fried.
  - Light soy sauce: 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine: 2.5ml (about 0.5 tablespoon)
  - Five-spice powder: 5g (about 2 teaspoons), or thirteen-spice powder: 2.5-5g (about 1-2 teaspoons)
  - (Optional) Ground cumin: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Ground white pepper: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)

- Honey-glazed roasted chicken wings: Using a single serving of 250g of bone-in wing flats as an example. Make a few slashes in the wings. The finished dish is sweet and salty, with sweetness taking the lead, and is roasted.
  - Light soy sauce: 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine: 2.5ml (about 0.5 tablespoon)
  - White granulated sugar: 5-15g (about 2-6 teaspoons, depending on your preferred sweetness)
  - Honey/syrup: 10-20ml (about 2-4 tablespoons, depending on your preferred sweetness. If using 10g or more of white granulated sugar, add only 10ml.)
  - (Optional) Five-spice powder: 2.5g (about 1 teaspoon. Do not substitute thirteen-spice powder.)

- Aromatic roasted salmon: Using a single serving of a 200g boneless salmon steak as an example. Do not cut up the fish; it is oven-roasted.
  - Light soy sauce: 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine: 2.5ml (about 0.5 tablespoon)
  - Brown sugar: 10-20g (about 4-8 teaspoons, depending on your preferred sweetness)
  - Italian balsamic vinegar/Zhenjiang aromatic black vinegar: 2.5-5ml (about 0.5-1 tablespoon, depending on your preferred sourness)
  - Ground nutmeg: 2.5g (about 1 teaspoon)
  - Ground thyme: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - Ground ginger: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - Ground rosemary: 1-2g (about 0.5-1 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Ground white pepper: 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Dried chile flakes: 2.5-10g (about 1-4 teaspoons, depending on your preferred heat level)
