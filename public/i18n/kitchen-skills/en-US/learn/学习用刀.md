# Learning to Use a Knife

This knife skills tutorial is for beginners in the home kitchen. The goal is steady cutting, appropriately sized pieces, cleanliness, and safety, not speed. Read the sections on safety and grip first, then choose the ingredients you need to prepare. If you encounter hard bones, tough shells, or rock-solid frozen food, switch tools or buy ingredients that have already been prepared.

## Before You Begin: Set Up Your Kitchen

1. Keep the counter well lit and clean, and clear enough space to work. Keep children and pets away from the work area. Stop using knives when you are tired, have been drinking alcohol, or cannot concentrate.
2. Wash and dry your hands. Check for a loose handle or chips in the blade, and stop using the knife if you find damage. Do not pick up a knife with greasy, wet, or slippery hands.
3. Choose an intact, flat cutting board made of wood or food-safe plastic. Place a nonslip mat or a wrung-out damp cloth underneath, then push the board with your hand to make sure it will not slide. Do not cut on glass, stone, or ceramic plates.
4. Arrange the ingredients to be cut, a bowl for prepared ingredients, and a bowl for scraps. Do not let the front edge of the cutting board extend beyond the counter. When not in use, lay the knife flat on the board, away from the edge, with the cutting edge facing a side where no one will touch it.
5. Use separate knives and cutting boards for raw meat and fish and for cooked foods and ready-to-eat fruits and vegetables. If you have only one set, prepare ready-to-eat foods first. After handling raw meat, wash everything thoroughly and disinfect it according to the cleaning product's instructions before switching tasks.
6. Cut only a small amount at a time. Take raw meat out of the refrigerator in batches, and refrigerate or cook it promptly after cutting. Do not leave it spread out at room temperature for a long time just to practice.

## Essential Safety Habits

- If a knife falls, step back. Never try to catch it or stop it with your foot. Wait until it has landed and stopped moving before picking it up.
- Do not point or gesture with a knife, or turn around to talk while holding one. Put the knife down before moving ingredients, answering the phone, or opening a cabinet.
- Do not hide a knife in dishwater, suds, or under a cloth or food. Do not leave knives loose in a drawer.
- Work in one place whenever possible. If you must move, keep the tip pointed down and the cutting edge away from yourself and others. Do not carry something heavy in your other hand at the same time, and do not squeeze through a crowd.
- To pass a knife, place it on a stable counter and wait for the other person to take it by the handle. Do not hand someone a knife tip-first.
- Do not use a kitchen knife to open cans, pry off bottle caps, turn screws, open hard shells, or open packaging. If the knife gets stuck, stop applying force. Do not twist it, strike its spine, or lift the food with the knife still in it and slam it against the board.
- A sharp knife can still injure you. Keeping it sharp helps reduce slipping and excessive force; it does not mean you can stop protecting your hands.
- Cut-resistant gloves cannot protect against every puncture or chopping injury. If you use them, choose a properly fitting, food-safe product and wash it as directed. You still need to use the correct grip.

## Types of Knives: Choose for the Task

| Tool | Good Uses | Unsuitable Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chinese slicing knife or thin Chinese vegetable knife | Slicing and julienning vegetables and boneless meat; the wide blade helps transfer ingredients | Chopping bones or cutting hard-frozen meat; a wide blade does not mean it can chop bones |
| Chef's knife | Everyday slicing, cutting into pieces, and mincing; the curved edge allows a small rocking motion | Prying open shells or twisting through hard bones; thin edges are especially vulnerable to damage |
| Santoku knife | Everyday preparation of vegetables, fish, and boneless meat | Forcing a rocking motion with the tip as a pivot or chopping bones forcefully |
| Paring knife or small fruit knife | Trimming small ingredients and removing stems; beginners should still work on a board | Quickly paring or cutting hard foods held in your palm |
| Serrated knife or bread knife | Cutting bread, ripe soft tomatoes, and similar foods with a gentle sawing motion | Chopping bones; when sawing with a long blade, keep your other hand especially far from its path |
| Boning knife or fillet knife | For experienced users to separate meat from bones or fillet fish; flexibility varies by intended use | Beginners holding meat in a bare hand and slicing toward the palm |
| Bone cleaver | Bone-cutting tasks expressly permitted by the manufacturer | Using it as an all-purpose knife; not every kind of bone can be handled at home |
| Kitchen shears | Snipping green onions, cutting packaging, and handling soft tissue as permitted by the manufacturer | Cutting hard bones beyond their rated use; cutting cooked food after use on raw food without washing them |

Beginners usually need only one main knife they can hold securely, one small knife, and a stable cutting board. Add a serrated knife if you eat bread often. You do not need a full knife set to get started. Check the intended uses first, then consider whether the length, weight, and handle fit your hand. A blade that is too long or too heavy is harder to control.

Stainless steel knives can rust too. Carbon steel knives need even more prompt drying; normal surface oxidation and discoloration are different from reddish-brown rust spots. Ceramic knives are hard but brittle and are unsuitable for twisting cuts, smashing garlic, or cutting bones or hard-frozen foods. Sharpening is usually best left to a professional.

## Knife Parts and Grips

### Your Knife Hand: Put Stability First

The spine is the thick edge opposite the cutting edge, the tip is at the front, and the heel is near the handle. The heel on some knives is also very sharp. Do not let your fingers curl underneath the cutting edge.

Handle grip: Wrap your fingers around the handle, keeping your thumb naturally tucked in and secure so your fingers cannot slide forward onto the blade. This grip is suitable when you are just starting to practice or when the knife's design does not suit a pinch grip.

Pinch grip: Gently pinch the two sides of the blade near the handle with your thumb and index finger, and wrap your remaining fingers around the handle. Keep your fingertips away from the cutting edge below. Use this grip when the knife's design allows it and it feels stable. Do not pinch the tip or cutting edge, or reach too far forward.

Grip just firmly enough to keep the knife from rotating; do not squeeze until your wrist becomes stiff. Keep your wrist as natural as possible and relax your shoulders. If an ingredient is too tall or too hard, change its shape first instead of raising your arm to apply more force.

### Your Guiding Hand: The Claw Grip Is Not Armor

1. Curl your fingertips toward your palm, using them to hold the food steady, with your knuckles facing the side of the blade.
2. Keep your thumb tucked behind the food, not in front of the cutting edge. Do not spread your pinky finger out either.
3. The side of the blade may rest lightly against the knuckles of your curled fingers as a position guide, but the cutting edge must always stay below the guiding knuckles. Do not lift the knife above them.
4. After each cut, let the knife come to a complete stop before moving your guiding hand back a little. Do not move your hand along in pursuit of the blade.
5. Stop when only a very short piece remains. Lay it flat and turn it in another direction, or save it for another use. You do not need to cut down to the last millimeter.

For slippery meat, round ingredients, or small pieces you cannot hold securely with your fingers, first blot off excess surface moisture, create a flat base, or switch tools. Do not force a cut while relying on the claw grip alone.

## Basic Cuts and Sizes

These dimensions are learning aids, not universal standards. Follow the specific recipe and its cooking requirements. Try to keep pieces of the same ingredient in one pot a similar size so they do not burn on the outside while remaining hard inside.

| Cut | Method | Common Uses and Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slicing | Rest the flat base on the board, cut steadily, and move your guiding hand back with each cut | For stir-fried potato slices, start practicing at about 2—3 millimeters thick; adjust the thickness to the recipe |
| Cutting into strips or julienning | Slice first, then stack a few slices evenly or spread them out before cutting into long strips | Beginners should start with thick strips and work up to fine julienne as their skills improve |
| Dicing | Turn the strips and cut across them | Start practicing with about 1-centimeter dice for side ingredients; do not stack too many layers at once |
| Cutting into chunks | Divide into similarly sized pieces | Stew ingredients are usually cut larger than those for a quick stir-fry; follow the recipe |
| Roll cutting | Cut a long ingredient on the diagonal, rotate it, and make another diagonal cut | Carrots and eggplant; before each rotation, lift the knife edge clear and bring the knife to a complete stop |
| Mincing | Cut into thin slices, fine strips, and small dice, then finely chop a small amount at a time | Ginger, garlic, and herbs; keep your guiding hand away from the cutting edge |
| Pull cutting or push cutting | Move the blade horizontally as it cuts into the food instead of repeatedly pressing straight down with force | Boneless meat and softer vegetables; the blade's range of motion must not reach your other hand |
| Gentle sawing | Move a serrated knife back and forth while minimizing downward pressure | Bread and soft tomatoes, to keep them from being crushed |

A rocking cut is suitable only when the knife design, space, and ingredients all allow it. Move the handle in a small arc, maintain control, and do not aim for continuous rapid chopping. A straight-edged knife does not need to mimic the motion of a curved chef's knife.

Use a bench scraper or your hands to transfer ingredients whenever possible. If you use the side of the blade, stop the knife first and keep the cutting edge away from your fingers. Avoid scraping the cutting edge sideways across the board, which dulls it. Do not run your fingers along the cutting edge to wipe off stuck food.

## Cutting Vegetables: Start with a Stable, Flat Surface

### Potatoes, Carrots, and Daikon: Create a Flat Base, Then Slice, Julienne, or Dice

1. Wash, peel as needed, and remove inedible parts. Do not practice with or eat potatoes that are green, noticeably sprouted, or spoiled.
2. First cut cylindrical carrots into manageable lengths if needed. Cut off a thin slice to create a flat base, then place that base down before continuing.
3. Start with slices of similar thickness. For julienne, stack only a few stable slices; if they slip, spread them out and cut one at a time.
4. To dice, first make strips of equal width, then cut across them. Do not stand a long radish upright and chop down through it.

![Create a flat base on round ingredients, then lay them flat to slice, cut into strips, and dice](images/knife-cuts.svg)

### Onions: Beginners Do Not Need to Cut Horizontally Toward Their Palm

1. Remove the skin and dry top, then cut in half along the top-to-root axis. Stabilize the onion before the first cut, shaving off a small flat surface if necessary.
2. Place an onion half cut-side down, with the root end toward the back. Make several cuts from the top toward the root, leaving a small connection near the root end.
3. Turn it and cut crosswise to dice, taking advantage of the onion's natural layers. There is no need to add horizontal cuts toward your palm.
4. Stop near the root end. Lay the remaining end flat to handle it separately, or discard it if it is difficult to hold steady.

Do not cut onions with your eyes closed. If tears interfere with your vision, put the knife down and step away from the cutting board. Continue when your vision has recovered.

### Green Peppers, Bell Peppers, and Chili Peppers

Stabilize the pepper, split it lengthwise, and lay it flat before removing the seeds and white ribs, then cut into strips or dice. If the skin slips, turn the inside face up. Avoid touching your eyes after handling chili peppers. You can use food-safe gloves; wash your hands and clean the knife and cutting board when finished.

### Tomatoes, Eggplant, and Cucumbers

Use a gentle pulling motion with a sharp, thin-edged knife on tomatoes, or saw gently with a serrated knife. Never suddenly apply more force because the blade slips. Remove the stem ends from cucumbers and eggplant, then cut lengthwise to create a stable base or halve them and lay them flat. For roll cuts, work with a small amount, stopping the knife before each rotation. Do not let the food roll toward the cutting edge.

### Leafy Greens, Green Onions, and Herbs

Wash first, drain off excess water, and remove bad leaves. Stack a small amount of leafy greens, roll them loosely, and cut into sections or strips. Do not roll them into a tall pile. Cut green onions into shorter lengths before lining them up to cut; use fewer at a time if the white parts are slippery. Gather herbs before chopping. Do not reach in to pick up stray leaves while bringing the knife down.

### Ginger, Garlic, and Mushrooms

Create a flat base on ginger, then cut it into slices, strips, and finally mince it. Peel garlic and slice or mince it, or use a garlic press. Beginners should not imitate forceful garlic-smashing with the side of a knife, especially with ceramic knives or thin, brittle blades. Remove unwanted stem ends from mushrooms. Halve large ones and place them cut-side down; if small ones are hard to hold steady, leave them whole and cook them according to the recipe.

### Harder Ingredients Such as Pumpkin, Winter Melon, and Corn

For large pieces of pumpkin and hard-skinned squash or melons, stability and the right tool matter most. Beginners should favor precut pieces. Do not strike the knife's spine, step on the knife, or raise it and slam it against the board to get through the food. If the knife becomes stuck after entering the food, stop. Do not twist the blade from side to side.

You can use a dedicated corn stripper to remove kernels. If using a knife, first make a short, stable section that can sit flat. Hold it securely at a low height, keep the cutting edge away from the hand holding it, and slice the kernels off one side at a time. If you cannot hold it securely, buy corn kernels ready to use. Do not stand a whole ear of corn high upright on a slippery counter to cut off the kernels.

## Cutting Meat: Check Both the Grain and Whether It Has Bones

### Boneless Beef and Pork: Slice Against the Grain

The long, thin, parallel muscle fibers form the grain. Cutting against the grain means cutting across those fibers to shorten the long fibers in each bite. It does not mean always cutting in a particular direction relative to your body.

1. Thaw raw meat in the refrigerator. Do not cut hard-frozen meat or leave it at room temperature for a long time waiting for it to soften.
2. Blot off excess surface juices, use a knife and cutting board reserved for raw meat, and promptly place cut meat in a container.
3. Identify the direction of the fibers and position the meat so you can easily cut across them. If it has no flat base, first divide it into pieces that can lie flat and stable.
4. Use steady pull cuts to make slices of similar thickness. For stir-fried meat, start practicing at about 2—4 millimeters thick, adjusting to the recipe. Do not hold meat taut in the air and cut toward your hand.
5. To cut into strips, slice first, then cut the slices into strips. For stews, cut into similarly sized chunks. The fiber direction may change in different parts of the meat, so turn the piece and check again.

![Muscle fibers run parallel, with the cut crossing the fibers to shorten their length](images/knife-grain.svg)

Rules of thumb such as "cut beef crosswise and pork lengthwise" cannot replace examining the particular cut and following the recipe. Whether to cut against the grain, how thick to slice, and whether pieces need to hold their shape all depend on the finished dish. For boneless meat that needs to be tender in a quick stir-fry, shortening the fibers usually makes it easier to chew.

### Chicken Breast, Chicken Thighs, and Bone-In Poultry

Lay chicken breast flat, remove any obvious connective tissue, and cut into slices or chunks. Beginners should not press a palm on the meat while slicing horizontally to make thin cutlets; you can buy thinly sliced meat instead. Choose boneless chicken thighs when possible, unfold them, and cut into pieces. Do not force a slicing knife through joints, leg bones, or frozen chicken pieces. Ask the seller to cut them for you.

Do not rinse raw chicken, as splashing can contaminate the surrounding area. After cutting raw poultry, wash your hands, knives, cutting boards, counters, and any handles you touched. Do not put cooked meat directly on a plate that held raw meat. Thinly sliced meat is not automatically safely cooked; cooking still needs to follow the recipe and food safety requirements.

### Fish, Shrimp, and Seafood

Start with boneless fish fillets when possible. Blot off excess moisture, lay the fillet flat, and use gentle pull cuts without crushing the flesh. If you find bones, remove them with fish bone tweezers and check the fillet. Do not skip checking just because it is labeled "boneless."

Deboning and skinning whole fish and opening shellfish involve slippery surfaces, sharp spines, and special tools; you can ask the seller to do this. Use kitchen shears to open the back of shrimp, then pick out the parts that need removing. Do not hold shrimp in your palm and stab toward your hand with a pointed knife. Do not force open oysters, coconuts, crab shells, or similar items with an ordinary kitchen knife.

### Cooked Meat and Leftovers

Cut cooked meat with a clean knife and cutting board that have not touched raw meat, slicing according to the grain and the recipe's needs. Do not press your hand hard onto hot meat straight from the pan. Let it reach a temperature you can handle before cutting, while avoiding a long stay at room temperature. Properly refrigerate cut leftovers promptly.

## Fruit, Bread, Tofu, and Other Ingredients

### Apples, Pears, and Citrus

Wash and cut them on the board. Create a flat surface on apples and pears first, cut into wedges, then lay them flat to remove the cores. Do not cradle a wedge in your hand and cut out the core toward your palm. Peel citrus by hand when possible. If slicing it, first create a stable base.

### Avocados, Mangoes, and Fruit with Pits

For ripe avocados, consider using a dedicated tool first. If using a knife, stabilize the fruit on the cutting board and slowly cut around the pit. Stop the knife, separate the halves with your hands, then use a spoon to remove the pit and flesh. Do not hold half an avocado in your palm and strike the pit with a knife or pry it out with the tip.

Create a stable base on a mango, then cut off the flesh on both sides of the flat pit. To cut small pieces, remove the flesh from the skin and put it on the board before cutting. Do not hold the skin in your hand and score a grid, allowing the knife tip to pierce the skin and stab your hand. After cutting peaches, plums, and similar fruit open, separate them by hand or remove the pit with a spoon. Do not force it out with a knife.

### Watermelon, Pineapple, and Hard-Shelled Fruit

For large watermelons and pineapples, first consider their weight and the size of your cutting board. Buy precut portions if you cannot control them. If you can handle them steadily, create a flat base first, divide them into smaller pieces, then trim off the rind or skin. Do not forcefully chop sideways into a whole large fruit that is rolling around. Hard shells such as coconuts require special tools and working conditions; an ordinary kitchen knife is unsuitable.

### Bread, Cake, Tofu, and Cheese

Saw bread gently with a serrated knife, keeping the hand holding it away from the knife's back-and-forth path. Reduce pressure as you reach the bottom to avoid suddenly cutting through. Cut cake steadily with a clean, long knife, then stop the knife before wiping off anything stuck to it.

Place tofu gently on the board and cut it into pieces with a thin, sharp knife without squeezing it with your hand. If cheese sticks to the blade, cut thinner slices or use a suitable cheese knife. Do not twist the knife to pry a large piece apart. Use a dedicated nutcracker for hard nutshells, and do not chop ice cubes or hard candy with a kitchen knife either.

## Washing, Drying, and Storage

1. Handle knives separately as soon as you finish using them. Hold the handle and keep the cutting edge away from your body and your other hand.
2. Wash with warm water, mild detergent, and a cleaning tool that is unlikely to scratch the blade. Clean in a direction away from the cutting edge. Do not wrap a sponge or cloth around the edge and slide it back and forth, or wipe the edge with your bare hand.
3. After knives and cutting boards are contaminated by raw food, remove debris and wash them first, then follow the directions for an appropriate disinfectant. Follow the product's requirements for concentration, contact time, and whether rinsing is needed. Do not mix cleaning products at will.
4. After rinsing, carefully dry the blade faces, the joint between the blade and handle, and the handle, keeping clear of the cutting edge. Do not soak knives for a long time. Dishwasher use is not recommended, especially for knives with wooden handles, carbon steel knives, or knives whose manufacturers prohibit machine washing.
5. Once completely dry, store knives in sheaths, a knife block, or a drawer with individual slots. Keep the knife block clean and dry too. A magnetic rack must be securely mounted and out of children's reach. Place and remove knives as directed by the manufacturer to avoid striking the cutting edge.

Care for wooden handles according to the manufacturer's instructions, using care products approved for food contact. Do not apply industrial machine oil on your own. Wash and dry carbon steel knives promptly after cutting acidic foods. Follow the instructions on whether to apply a thin coat of food-safe rust-preventive oil for long-term storage, and clean the knife before use. If you find rust, cracks, a loose handle, or obvious chips, have the knife repaired or replace it before using it again.

## Sharpening, Honing, and Checking Sharpness

### When Maintenance Is Needed

Needing more force for the same ingredients, repeatedly slipping on tomatoes, or crushing green onion leaves instead of cutting through them may indicate that the edge needs maintenance. Also check whether you are using the wrong knife, the cutting board is slipping, or the ingredient lacks a flat base.

Do not test a blade by running a finger along the edge, shaving hair, or cutting paper held in your hand. Check whether it cuts smoothly using normal ingredients and normal cutting techniques on the board. If the edge is chipped, do not keep making test cuts.

### Honing Rods vs. Whetstones

Some steel honing rods primarily help realign slightly displaced edges. Ceramic and diamond rods may also remove metal. Different blade materials, hardness levels, and edge geometries require different tools. You cannot simply apply another knife's maintenance method to a thin-edged Japanese knife, a single-bevel knife, or a ceramic knife.

Whetstones restore an edge by removing a small amount of material. There is no single angle that suits every knife. Give priority to the manufacturer's instructions for the angle per side, edge geometry, and sharpening tools. Mistaking the total included angle for the angle on one side will damage the edge.

### A Beginner's Maintenance Routine

1. Read the knife's instructions to confirm whether you can sharpen it yourself, which sharpening tools are suitable, and what angle to use. If you are unsure, use a professional sharpening service.
2. Secure the whetstone or guided sharpener on a nonslip base, with a clean counter and adequate lighting. Follow the sharpening tool's instructions on soaking, adding water, or using oil. Do not soak every whetstone in water.
3. Use a guided tool or a steady motion you have already learned, applying light pressure and keeping your fingers out of the blade's path at all times. Do not hold a honing rod in midair and show off with rapid knife strokes.
4. Do not press hard or grind the edge excessively thin in pursuit of sharpness. Have serrated knives, single-bevel knives, and ceramic knives maintained by someone familiar with that particular knife type.
5. When finished, clean metal filings from the knife and counter, dry everything, and put it away. A sharpened knife will feel different, so start slowly with a small amount of food.

Sharpening frequency depends on how heavily the knife is used, the blade material, the cutting board, and your technique. There is no need to sharpen on a fixed schedule. Using a suitable cutting board, avoiding sideways scraping with the edge, and storing knives separately are often more important than frequent sharpening.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

| Problem | Check First | A More Controlled Approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food rolls around | No flat base or an overly slippery board | Stop the knife, create a flat base, and secure the cutting board |
| The knife slips on the skin | A dull blade, wet surface, or unsuitable knife | Blot off excess moisture and switch to a sharp knife or a suitable serrated knife |
| Slices vary in thickness | Too many stacked layers, inconsistent guiding-hand movement, or cutting too fast | Reduce the amount, stop after each cut, and start practicing with thick slices |
| Meat gets crushed | A dull blade, overly soft meat, or repeatedly pressing straight down with force | Keep it firm by refrigerating it, and use a sharp knife with steady pull cuts |
| The knife gets stuck in a hard ingredient | The task exceeds the knife's capabilities | Stop twisting, prying, or striking it; buy precut ingredients or ask the seller to prepare them |
| Your wrist aches | A knife that is too heavy, an unsuitable counter height, or an overly tight grip | Stop and rest, adjust your tools and posture, and seek medical care promptly if pain persists |
| Food sticks to the blade | A moist or starchy cut surface | Stop the knife and use a suitable tool to remove the food; do not wipe along the cutting edge |

## Practice in Stages: No Timing or Racing

1. In the first stage, practice only setting up, gripping the knife, using the claw grip, and stopping the knife. Choose half a cucumber that is easy to hold steady or a vegetable that is neither too hard nor too soft, and cut a few thick slices.
2. In the second stage, practice slices, strips, and dice with a potato that has a flat base. Check that the pieces are similar in size; do not aim for hair-thin cuts.
3. In the third stage, practice diced onion, pepper strips, and sections of leafy greens, learning how to stabilize different ingredients.
4. In the fourth stage, practice slicing boneless meat with a separate knife and cutting board for raw meat. Identify the grain first, cut a small amount, then finish by cleaning and disinfecting.
5. Review after each session: Did the board move? Was your thumb exposed? Did you force the knife down? Did you deliberately stop when you reached a small end piece? Did you put the knife away promptly?

At every stage, move on only when you can work steadily at a slow pace. Minors should receive in-person guidance from an adult with the necessary skills, using tools and tasks suited to their age and ability.

## If You Accidentally Cut Yourself

Put the knife down immediately and stop handling food. For a small wound, apply continuous direct pressure with a clean dressing or cloth to stop the bleeding. If an object is embedded in the wound, do not remove it yourself or press directly on it. Apply pressure around it and seek medical care. If the dressing becomes soaked, add another layer on top rather than repeatedly lifting it to look.

Once bleeding has stopped, rinse a small, superficial wound with clean running water, clean the surrounding skin, and cover it with a sterile dressing. Discard food contaminated by the wound and clean contaminated counters. Do not put salt, coffee grounds, or food-based home remedies on the wound.

Seek immediate medical help for bleeding that cannot be controlled, spurting blood, a deep or large wound, a serious palm injury, numbness, limited movement, or a foreign object in the wound. In an emergency in mainland China, call 120. Seek medical care if increasing redness or swelling, pus, fever, or similar symptoms develop afterward, so a healthcare professional can assess infection and the need for tetanus prevention. A written tutorial cannot replace in-person first aid training.
