# Blanching

Blanching is a step in cooking, called chāo shuǐ in Chinese.

Blanching means placing ingredients that have undergone initial preparation into a pot of boiling water, heating them until partially or fully cooked, and removing them for further cooking or seasoning.

Blanching is an essential step in cooking, especially for cold-tossed dishes. It plays a key role in a dish's color, aroma, and flavor, particularly its color.

Most vegetables and meat ingredients with fishy or gamey odors need to be blanched.

## Procedure

### Blanching in Boiling Water

To blanch in boiling water, heat the water in the pot first, then add the ingredients. Turn them promptly after adding them, keep the blanching time short, and do not overcook them.

This method is mostly used for plant ingredients, such as celery, spinach, and celtuce. Pay particular attention to heat and timing when blanching. If the ingredients cook even a little too long, their color will fade and they will lose their crispness and tenderness. After adding them to the pot, remove them as soon as the water returns to a gentle boil and let them cool.

- Blanch leafy vegetables before slicing them to avoid excessive nutrient loss.
- Use plenty of water and high heat when blanching so the water can return to a boil quickly after the ingredients are added. When blanching leafy green vegetables, remove them after just a brief boil.
- Immediately cool and drain vegetables after blanching to prevent residual heat from turning them yellow or making them overcooked and mushy.
- When blanching vegetables, you can add an appropriate amount of salad oil, such as peanut oil, corn oil, or soybean oil, to keep them bright green.

### Blanching from Cold Water

To blanch from cold water, put the ingredients and cold water in the pot together. The water should cover the ingredients. Bring it to a boil to cook the ingredients and make further preparation easier.

Potatoes, carrots, and similar ingredients take longer to cook because they are large and do not cook through easily.

Some animal ingredients, such as plain boiled pork, beef omasum tripe, and beef tripe collar, are also started in cold water, heated until cooked, and then prepared further. Some animal ingredients used for soup should also be started in cold water so their nutrients are gradually released during heating, making the broth flavorful. Starting them in hot water, on the other hand, causes their proteins to coagulate.

- Do not add too much water to the pot; use just enough to cover the ingredients.
- Turn the ingredients frequently as they gradually heat up so they heat evenly and achieve the purpose of blanching.

## Additional Things to Keep in Mind

- Blanching sometimes also causes unstable, water-soluble nutrients to leach out of ingredients. Water-soluble vitamins in fresh vegetables are particularly susceptible to loss
- Blanch animal ingredients separately from plant ingredients, ingredients with strong colors and flavors separately from those with mild colors and flavors, and large pieces separately from small pieces to keep their flavors from transferring to one another
- After blanching animal ingredients, the liquid can be skimmed and clarified for use as stock

### Blanching Meat

- Once meat changes color in boiling water, it is ready to be removed. Drain it, then proceed to the next cooking step.
- After blanching meat, wash off any clinging blood foam and residue. Remember to use warm water; otherwise, thermal expansion and contraction will cause the meat to trap the residue, making the blood foam impossible to wash off completely.

### Blanching Leafy Greens

- Sprinkle some salt into the water when washing leafy greens to help wash out any insects
- Immediately immerse blanched greens in cold water to preserve their color and texture. Without a cold-water soak, the residual heat from the boiling water will make the greens lose their crispness and turn mushy
