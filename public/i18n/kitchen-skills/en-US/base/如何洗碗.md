# How to Wash Dishes

## Dishwashing in the 21st Century

Washing dishes seems like the simplest kitchen chore. As modern kitchen equipment becomes increasingly complex, however, “washing dishes” has long since expanded beyond its literal meaning. Yet many people still think of it in terms of the “old-fashioned” approach of “scrubbing dishes with a cloth.” In some households, improper dishwashing habits over the years have even created hidden health risks, such as carcinogens. To help everyone learn sound, efficient, modern dishwashing methods, this guide explains the process, precautions, and common pitfalls in detail.

## The Purpose of Washing Dishes

Whether you wash by hand or use a dishwasher, there is only one ultimate goal: to return your dishes and utensils to a condition in which they are **safe to use again**. Specifically, all of the following conditions must be met:

1. **They look clean**: No oily film, water spots, food scraps, or fingerprints on the surface.
2. **They feel clean**: Run a finger along the bottom of a bowl, a spoon handle, or the inside rim of a cup. If there is no sticky residue, it is clean.
3. **They smell clean**: No rancid oil smell or harsh detergent odor.
4. **They are dry and ready to use**: After draining or towel-drying, there is no obvious standing water, and they are not damp when put away.
5. **There is no cross-contamination**: Tools used to handle raw meat have not contaminated dishes that have already been washed.

Only when all 5 conditions are met are the dishes truly clean.

## Tools to Have Ready

Modern kitchens contain cookware and dishes made from many materials: nonstick coatings, cast iron, enamel, wood, stainless steel, and more. Different materials require different cleaning tools, and choosing the wrong tool is itself a common pitfall. Basic kitchen setup is assumed to include dishwashing supplies. When washing dishes, make sure the following items are available:

```text
Hot water (preferred), cold water, sink, optional dishpan, dish sponge or scouring pad
Dish detergent, steel wool (stainless steel cookware only; never on nonstick or coated surfaces)
Drying rack, clean dishcloth or paper towels, optional gloves
Optional dishwasher; see below
```

### Key Points for Using Your Tools

- **Sponge/scouring pad**: The first choice for everyday bowls and plates. Squeeze it dry after use and leave it in a ventilated place to dry so it does not become moldy or smelly.
- **Steel wool**: Use only on hard, uncoated surfaces, such as iron and stainless steel pans. **Never** use it on nonstick cookware, ceramic coatings, or fine china, as it will damage the coating.
- **Dishcloths**: Keep cloths used to dry dishes separate from those used to wipe the stove or floor. Using them interchangeably undoes the washing.
- **Dishwasher**: If you have one, you can skip the main handwashing process, but you still need to scrape off food scraps beforehand and put dishes away afterward. See the “Dishwasher” section below.

## The Overall Process

Many people wash dishes in just two steps: “apply detergent → rinse.” That is far from enough. A sound dishwashing routine should include the following stages:

```text
Collect and sort → Scrape and soak → Wash and degrease → Rinse away detergent → Inspect → Dry → Store
```

Each step serves an essential purpose, and skipping any of them can leave hidden problems. The steps are explained below.

## Detailed Steps

### 1. Gather and Sort

After a meal, gather the bowls, plates, chopsticks, spoons, pots, cutting boards, and other items scattered around the kitchen beside the sink. Sort them by how dirty they are.

Wash in the following order, from least to most soiled:

1. **Lightly soiled**: Drinking glasses, cups, chopsticks, and spoons
2. **Moderately soiled**: Serving plates and rice bowls
3. **Heavily soiled**: Stir-fry pans, deep-frying pots, baking sheets, and containers with raw egg residue
4. **High-risk items**: Cutting boards and knives used for raw meat or fish, preferably handled separately at the end

Why sort them? Washing lightly soiled items first keeps the water and sponge relatively clean and prevents heavy grease from getting onto cups. Washing tools used for raw food separately at the end keeps pathogens from reaching dishes that are already clean.

### 2. Scrape and Soak

Many people skip this step, but it directly determines how difficult the rest of the washing will be. Loosening stubborn residue beforehand saves repeated scrubbing later, reducing both effort and water use.

What to do:

1. Use a paper napkin or spatula to scrape off large food scraps and put them in the food waste bin. **Do not rinse them straight down the drain**, as they can cause clogs.
2. Soak items with the following:
   - Dried-on rice grains or starchy paste
   - Set protein residue, such as egg or milk
   - Scorched or burned-on food on the bottom of a pan
3. Soaking methods:
   - **Ordinary grease and rice grains**: Soak in hot water for 5–15 minutes.
   - **Scorched pan bottoms**: Soak in hot water with a little dish detergent, or add water, bring it to a boil, turn off the heat, and let it sit covered for 10–20 minutes.
   - **Tea stains and mineral deposits on stainless steel**: Soak with a little white vinegar, then scrub. Do not mix it with bleach products.

> [!Warning]
> **Do not run cold water directly into a pan containing oil before it has cooled**. Thermal expansion and contraction can warp the pan, and cold water hitting the oil can cause splattering and burns.
> Do not soak **cast iron or carbon steel pans** for long periods, as they rust easily. Dry them immediately after washing and apply a thin coat of oil.

### 3. Wash: Remove Grease and Grime

This is the core of the entire process. Warm to hot water, around 40–50°C, is recommended. Hot water effectively reduces the viscosity of grease, making cleaning much more efficient. If your faucet has adjustable temperature control, set it directly to a suitable temperature.

Standard handwashing steps:

1. Turn on the faucet, or fill a dishpan with warm water.
2. Squeeze a small amount of dish detergent onto the sponge, about the size of a mung bean to a soybean, or approximately 1–2 ml, and work it into a lather.
3. Scrub in the order “outside → inside, handle → working end, edges → center”:
   - **Bowls**: Scrub the outside first, then the inside, paying particular attention to the inside bottom.
   - **Plates**: Wash the eating surface, rim, and underside.
   - **Chopsticks and spoons**: Focus on the tips of chopsticks and the bowls of spoons, where most residue collects.
   - **Pots and pans**: Wash the bottom, sides, and rim, and remember any grease splattered on the outside.
4. For stubborn stains, start by applying extra pressure to the spot with the rough side of the sponge. Do not reach straight for steel wool.
5. Cutting boards and knives:
   - Cutting boards used for raw meat: After scrubbing with detergent, you can scald them with hot water. Disinfect them regularly when possible.
   - Knives: Scrub with the cutting edge facing away from you to avoid cuts.

More detergent does not mean cleaner dishes. Using too much only makes rinsing harder, and leaving detergent residue on dishes is undesirable in itself.

### 4. Rinse: Remove the Detergent

After washing, thoroughly rinse away the detergent and loosened grime with clean water.

What to do:

1. Rinse each item thoroughly under clean running water until all of the following are true:
   - No suds remain.
   - It does not feel slippery or greasy to the touch.
   - Water forms an even film on the surface. If grease remains, water will bead up instead of spreading evenly.
2. Detergent tends to linger around cup rims, in the recessed undersides of bowls, and in the joints around pan handles. Rinse these areas for a few extra seconds.
3. If you rinse in a dishpan of standing water, change the water at least once, then give everything a final rinse under running water.

### 5. Inspect

Check each item:

- **Look**: Hold it up to the light and check for reflections from an oily film.
- **Feel**: Gently touch the inside surface with a fingertip to check for stickiness.
- **Smell**: Sniff it up close for sour or detergent odors.

If an item fails any check, return to Step 3 and rewash the affected area. Do not leave unclean spots and move straight to drying.

### 6. Dry

Clean does not mean ready to use immediately: moisture provides a breeding ground for bacteria. Many people think the job is finished once the dishes are washed, but putting them away before they are dry can undo that effort.

Options:

| Method | How to Do It | Best For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air-dry | Place upside down on a drying rack and let air-dry naturally | The most common everyday option; requires little effort |
| Towel-dry | Wipe with a clean cloth reserved for dishes until no visible droplets remain | Putting dishes away right away; preventing water spots on glasses |
| Heated-air drying | Use the dishwasher's drying cycle or warm air | Drying a large batch |

Notes:

- When draining dishes, place them **upside down or on their sides** whenever possible to keep water from collecting in recessed areas.
- If the cloth already smells or feels damp and slimy, wash it before using it to dry dishes.
- Do not dry bowls with a cloth you just used to wipe the stove.

### 7. Put Away

Once you have confirmed that the dishes are dry, return them to the cabinet or dish rack.

A few basic rules:

1. **Do not put dishes away while wet**: Damp dishes in a cabinet encourage bacteria and odors.
2. Before stacking bowls or plates, make sure their undersides are clean. Do not rest a dirty underside against the inside of the bowl below it.
3. Store chopsticks, spoons, and forks with their eating ends up, or follow the orientation intended by your utensil drying basket, keeping them well ventilated.
4. Store knives and cutting boards used for raw food separately from everyday dishes and utensils.

## Recommended Washing Order

When washing a full set of dishes and cookware, use the following order:

```text
1. Drinking glasses and cups
2. Chopsticks, spoons, and forks
3. Plates and bowls
4. Spatulas, ladles, and other utensils
5. Woks and saucepans with heavy grease
6. Raw-food cutting boards and knives (last, then wash the sink separately)
7. Rinse the sink and faucet handles to finish
```

The reasoning is to progress from less to more contaminated items, preventing heavy grease from later items from dirtying things you have already washed.

## Cleaning Different Materials and Kitchen Items

Modern kitchens routinely contain cookware and dishes made from many different materials, but their cleaning requirements vary considerably. The wrong method can damage an item or even create a health risk.

### Nonstick Pans

- Gently wipe with the soft side of a sponge and a pH-neutral detergent.
- **Never use steel wool or scrape the coating with a hard spatula**, as this will destroy the coating and ruin the pan.
- Dry after washing, and do not leave water standing in the pan for long periods.

### Cast Iron / Carbon Steel Pans

- Avoid detergent or use as little as possible to protect the layer of oil on the surface.
- Use hot water and a brush to remove food residue.
- After washing, wipe dry, heat over low heat to dry completely, and apply a thin layer of cooking oil to prevent rust.

### Wooden Cutting Boards / Wooden Spatulas

- Wash quickly. Do not soak for long periods, as the wood can crack and warp.
- Stand upright to air-dry after washing.
- Periodically rub with cooking oil to maintain the wood (cutting boards).

### Chopsticks

- Focus on the ends that touch food: the tips.
- Bamboo and wooden chopsticks can retain water and crack, so be sure to air-dry them after washing.
- Replace them if they become badly moldy or cracked. Do not keep using them anyway.

### Dishwasher

A dishwasher is one of the most effective tools for improving efficiency in a modern kitchen, but it is not a magic machine where you can simply “throw the dishes in.” It still needs some help from you to work at its best:

1. Scrape off large food scraps, such as bones and vegetable leaves, first. Otherwise, they can clog the filter and spray holes.
2. Load dishes according to the dishwasher's manual, with bowls facing down and plates standing on edge. Make sure nothing blocks the spray arms from rotating.
3. Choose an appropriate cycle: heavy-duty or high-temperature for greasy loads, and quick wash or eco mode for lightly soiled everyday loads.
4. Open the dishwasher after the cycle and inspect the dishes, especially the recessed undersides of bowls and the insides of cup rims. Touch up anything still dirty by hand.
5. Clean the dishwasher filter and seals regularly to prevent odors and mold. Clean the filter every 1–2 weeks and run an empty cycle with a dedicated dishwasher cleaner once a month.

### Air Fryer

The basket and bottom tray are the main parts that need cleaning. Many people give them a quick wipe and put them back, but grease buildup will eventually carbonize, affecting heating performance and food safety.

- After each use, let the appliance cool, then remove the basket and bottom tray and wash them with warm water, dish detergent, and a sponge.
- Food scraps tend to lodge in the basket's mesh openings. Use an old toothbrush or small brush to help remove them.
- **Do not scrub the basket with steel wool**. Most baskets have a nonstick coating, and steel wool will damage it, causing food to stick and the coating to peel during later use.
- Wipe grease splatters from the interior walls with a damp cloth. **Do not immerse the entire appliance in water**.
- If the heating element at the top has grease on it, let it cool and wipe gently with a damp cloth. Grease left uncleaned for a long time can smoke or even produce harmful substances at high temperatures.

### Rice Cooker

Most rice cooker inner pots have a nonstick coating that needs protection during cleaning. Many people overlook the steam valve and the underside of the lid.

- Wash the inner pot with the soft side of a sponge and dish detergent. **Never use steel wool**. Once the coating is scratched and starts peeling, rice will stick, and flakes of coating can get into the food.
- The underside of the lid, steam vent, and sealing ring are easy to overlook. Starchy steam condenses into sticky residue in these areas, which eventually turns sour and smelly. Wipe the underside of the lid after each use, and periodically remove and wash the sealing ring.
- Wipe the outer housing with a damp cloth only. **Do not immerse it in water**, as this can damage the electrical circuitry.

### Stovetop Pressure Cookers / Electric Pressure Cookers

The most important parts to clean on a pressure cooker are not the pot itself, but the safety-related components: the sealing ring, steam release valve, and lid. Inadequate cleaning of these parts affects hygiene and can also compromise the seal, creating safety risks.

- **Sealing ring**: Remove after each use and wash with warm water and dish detergent. Food scraps and grease easily collect in its grooves, so scrub them carefully. Let it air-dry before reinstalling. Replace the ring promptly when it deteriorates, becoming hard, misshapen, or cracked.
- **Steam release valve / pressure-regulating valve**: Remove and rinse with clean water, making sure the valve opening is clear. If food scraps block the opening, pressure may not release properly the next time you use it.
- **Lid**: Scrub the underside and grooves around the edges, where grease and food residue tend to collect.
- **Inner pot**: Clean as you would an ordinary stainless steel or nonstick pot, choosing tools appropriate to its material.

### Oven

The baking sheets, racks, and interior walls all need regular cleaning. Many people wash only the baking sheets and overlook the racks and walls. Over time, carbonized grease produces smoky odors and can even affect the flavor of food.

- **Baking sheets**: Wash with dish detergent and a sponge while still warm, taking care not to burn yourself. Grease hardens as it cools and becomes harder to remove. For stubborn baked-on residue, soak in hot water for 15–30 minutes before scrubbing. Lining baking sheets with aluminum foil or parchment paper can make cleanup much easier.
- **Racks**: The gaps in wire racks are difficult to clean. Soak the racks in the sink with hot water and dish detergent, then scrub with a brush.
- **Interior walls**: Once cool, wipe with a damp cloth and a little dish detergent. If grease has carbonized and turned black, mix baking soda and water into a paste, apply it to the stains, and leave for 1–2 hours before wiping off. Do not scrape the oven walls with steel wool, as it can damage the surface coating.
- Some ovens have a self-cleaning function that uses high-temperature pyrolysis. Follow the manual, but still wipe away the remaining ash afterward.

### Microwave

The interior walls and turntable easily get dirty from splattered juices and grease. Many people leave them uncleaned, leading to odors over time. Old food scraps also heat up repeatedly during use and produce a burned smell.

- **Turntable**: Remove and wash like ordinary glassware or ceramic dishes.
- **Interior walls**: Wipe with a damp cloth and a little dish detergent. For dried-on food splatters, place a bowl of water with a few lemon slices in the microwave and heat on medium for 3–5 minutes. The steam will soften the residue and make wiping it away much easier.
- **Door seal**: The seal around the door frame is an easily overlooked spot. Grease and crumbs collect in its crevices, so wipe it regularly with a damp cloth.
- **Do not use cleaning tools containing metal** on the microwave's interior walls, as they can damage the coating.

### Steamer Pots / Steamer Inserts

The most common problems after using steamer pots and inserts are mineral deposits and starchy residue.

- **Stainless steel steamer inserts**: Wash normally with dish detergent and a sponge. Bits of steamed dough, such as the bottoms of steamed buns or filled buns, can lodge in the holes. Remove them promptly, as they become very difficult to remove once dry.
- **Bamboo steamer baskets**: Do not use detergent. Scrub with hot water and a brush. Bamboo absorbs detergent odors, which can transfer to food the next time you steam something. Always air-dry thoroughly in a ventilated place after washing to prevent mold. Discard moldy bamboo steamers.
- **Steamer pot**: Mineral deposits, which appear as white crystals, tend to collect on the bottom. Soak with white vinegar for 10–20 minutes, then scrub.

### Spatulas / Ladles / Skimmers

These tools are used every day, but cleaning methods differ depending on their material:

- **Stainless steel**: The most durable option. You can scrub it with steel wool without worrying about scratches. Pay attention to crevices where the handle joins the spoon or ladle, as grease easily collects there.
- **Silicone**: Wash with a sponge and dish detergent. Silicone withstands high temperatures but readily absorbs colors and odors, especially from curry and tomato sauce. If badly discolored, soak in a baking soda solution. Do not scrub silicone surfaces with steel wool, as it leaves scratches that trap more grime.
- **Wood**: As with wooden spatulas and cutting boards, wash and dry quickly without soaking. See “Wooden Cutting Boards / Wooden Spatulas” above.

### Vegetable Peelers

The gap between a peeler's blade and its mounting slot is tiny, so vegetable fibers and starch easily get trapped there. Many people simply rinse a peeler under water after use, leaving it incompletely cleaned.

- Use an old toothbrush to clean the gaps, brushing along the blade. Pay attention to the direction to avoid cuts.
- Shake off excess water after washing and leave in a ventilated place to air-dry. Even a stainless steel peeler's blade can rust and become dull if left wet for long periods.

### Stovetop Kettles / Electric Kettles

The main issue with kettles is mineral buildup rather than grease.

- **Removing mineral deposits**: Add white vinegar or citric acid, about 1–2 tablespoons, fill with water, bring to a boil, turn off the heat, and let soak for 30–60 minutes. Pour it out, then rinse with clean water 2–3 times. In areas with hard water, descale every 1–2 weeks.
- Do not scrub the inside with steel wool. It can scratch the stainless steel, and those scratches make it easier for mineral deposits to adhere.
- Mineral deposits also collect in the spout and on the underside of the lid, so clean those areas too.

## Common Problems and Pitfalls

Below are the most common dishwashing mistakes and misconceptions, organized by type. Each includes specific consequences. Understanding why you should avoid a practice helps you actually steer clear of these pitfalls.

### Washing Habits

- **Rinsing without scrubbing**: Water alone cannot remove an oily film. Grease does not dissolve in water. It must be emulsified by the surfactants in detergent and removed with the physical friction of a sponge. Rinsing without scrubbing leaves bowls looking clean but still coated in a thin, slippery layer of oil. The next time you serve rice in them, the food can pick up the smell of spoiled grease.
- **Using too much detergent**: More suds do not mean cleaner dishes. Beyond a certain concentration, adding detergent does not improve cleaning performance. Using too much makes it difficult to rinse everything off, and the residue will be swallowed with your next meal. Although long-term ingestion of detergent residue will not cause immediate poisoning, it irritates the lining of the digestive tract, and there is no need to take that risk.
- **Washing only the inside and ignoring the outside and bottom**: Many people scrub only the inside of a bowl, overlooking the outer walls and underside. When bowls and plates are stacked, the underside of the one on top directly touches the inside of the one below. If that underside is dirty, washing the lower bowl was wasted effort.
- **Drying before rinsing thoroughly**: Putting bowls on the drying rack while detergent suds remain leaves a thin, dried film of detergent on the surface. The next time you use them, it enters your body with the food. The test is simple: run a finger over the surface. If it still feels slippery, it has not been rinsed clean.
- **Leaving dirty dishes overnight**: Starch and protein residue left at room temperature overnight dries on firmly, requiring several times as much scrubbing the next day. More importantly, bacteria begin multiplying rapidly in food scraps after 2–4 hours at room temperature in summer. Dirty dishes left overnight are essentially a petri dish. Meat scraps can also attract cockroaches and fruit flies.

### Tool Use

- **Scrubbing nonstick pans with steel wool**: Steel wool scratches nonstick coatings, usually Teflon, and once damaged, the coating will continue to peel. A damaged nonstick pan loses its nonstick function, and flakes of coating can get into food. Once the coating is damaged and the pan underneath is exposed, food is also more likely to stick and burn. Use only the soft side of a sponge to clean nonstick cookware.
- **Wiping dishes with a dirty cloth or smelly sponge**: Sponges and cloths that stay damp without drying are themselves breeding grounds for bacteria and mold. “Washing” dishes with a smelly sponge amounts to spreading bacteria over them. The same applies to cloths: wiping bowls with a cloth just used to clean grease off the stove merely moves the dirt somewhere else. Squeeze sponges dry and leave them in a ventilated place after every use, and regularly wash cloths at high temperatures or replace them.
- **Scraping enamel or ceramic coatings with hard tools**: The surfaces of enameled and ceramic-coated cookware are relatively brittle. Forceful scraping with a metal spatula or steel wool can chip or scratch them. Chipped enamel exposes the underlying metal, affecting even heating and making the exposed metal prone to rust.
- **Leaving the faucet running at full blast throughout washing**: Keeping the water running the entire time can waste tens of liters in a single dishwashing session. A better approach is to turn off the water while scrubbing all the dishes with a sponge and detergent, then turn it on to rinse everything together.

### Material Damage

- **Soaking cast iron or carbon steel pans for long periods**: Cast iron and carbon steel have no rustproof coating, or rely on a layer of oil for rust protection, so prolonged soaking quickly causes rust. Rust is unsightly, and severe rusting also requires reseasoning and maintaining the pan's seasoning again. Dry immediately after washing, heat over low heat to dry completely, and apply a thin coat of cooking oil.
- **Using lots of detergent on cast iron or carbon steel pans**: Detergent washes away the layer of oil you have worked hard to build up, causing serious sticking the next time you cook. For everyday cleaning, cast iron and carbon steel pans need only hot water and a brush. Coarse salt can help scrub away stubborn residue.
- **Running cold water straight into a hot pan**: A pan is very hot after cooking, and cold water causes abrupt thermal expansion and contraction. Stainless steel pans can warp, and cast iron can even crack. If hot oil remains in the pan, adding cold water can also cause splattering and burns. Let the pan cool slightly before washing.
- **Soaking wooden cutting boards or spatulas for long periods**: Wood expands as it absorbs water and contracts as it dries. Repeating this a few times causes cracking and warping. Once a wooden cutting board develops cracks, food scraps and bacteria can hide inside, making it difficult to clean thoroughly. Wash and dry quickly, and stand upright to air-dry.

### Hygiene Risks

- **Putting wet bowls straight into a cabinet**: Placing dishes in an enclosed cabinet before they dry gives bacteria and mold an ideal warm, moist environment. Within a few days, mold spots or odors may appear on the bowls, potentially affecting all the dishes in the cabinet.
- **Washing tools used for raw food together with other dishes**: If cutting boards and knives used for raw meat or fish go straight into the dishwater with other dishes and utensils, pathogens that raw meat may carry, such as Salmonella and E. coli, can spread through the water to the other items. Wash tools used for raw food separately at the end, scalding with hot water or disinfecting when necessary.
- **Neglecting the drying rack**: If the drip tray beneath a drying rack is not emptied and cleaned regularly, standing water becomes smelly and moldy. Dirty water evaporating from below can then recontaminate dishes on the rack. Wash the drying rack and drip tray once a week.
- **Letting water collect at the bottom of a chopstick holder**: If chopsticks are stored tips-down in a holder without drainage holes, or one that is rarely cleaned, water can collect at the bottom and mold can grow. The tips touch food, so soaking them in dirty water means eating with moldy chopsticks every time. Choose a holder with drainage holes or store chopsticks tips-up.
- **Continuing to use moldy bamboo or wooden chopsticks**: Once black mold spots appear, mold has already penetrated the wood fibers and cannot be removed by ordinary washing. Some molds, such as Aspergillus flavus, produce potent carcinogens. Discard and replace moldy or cracked bamboo and wooden chopsticks.

### Other Easily Overlooked Pitfalls

- **Loading the dishwasher without scraping off food scraps**: Assuming the dishwasher can handle everything is a mistake. Large scraps, such as bones, vegetable leaves, and rice grains, can clog the filter and spray holes, reducing cleaning performance and, in severe cases, damaging the drain pump. At a minimum, scrape off visible solid food scraps before loading.
- **Neglecting dishwasher cleaning**: If the filter, seals, and spray arms go uncleaned for long periods, grease and mold accumulate. This can leave washed dishes smelling bad and turn the dishwasher itself into a source of contamination. Clean the filter every 1–2 weeks and run an empty cycle with a dedicated dishwasher cleaner once a month.
- **Rinsing food scraps straight down the drain**: Starchy scraps such as rice grains, noodles, and vegetable leaves can swell and clump in the drain, eventually blocking the pipes. Grease congeals on pipe walls and accelerates clogging. Scrape solid food scraps into the trash first and fit a strainer over the sink drain to catch stray bits.
- **Mixing detergent with chlorine bleach**: Some people add both detergent and 84 disinfectant, a chlorine bleach product, to dishwater for “double disinfection.” However, mixing them can produce harmful gases that irritate the respiratory tract. If disinfection is needed, first wash and rinse normally, then soak separately in a disinfectant solution and finish by rinsing with clean water.

## Conclusion

Cooking turns ingredients into something ready to eat; washing dishes restores the kitchen to a state in which you can cook again. Together, they form a complete cycle. If you cook without washing dishes, the kitchen will soon become unusable.

Learning sound dishwashing methods, making good use of modern kitchen tools, and understanding the proper care of different materials can make washing dishes both efficient and safe. Building the habit of “wash after use, put away after washing” helps make cooking sustainable over the long term.
