## Choosing Cooking Oil

Before choosing cooking oil, you need to know a few basics about fatty acids.

### Types of Fatty Acids

Fatty acids are classified as:

* Saturated fatty acids (avoid as much as possible)
* Unsaturated fatty acids
  * Cis fatty acids
  * Trans fatty acids (take particular care to avoid these)
  * Polyunsaturated fatty acids
  * Monounsaturated fatty acids

Saturated fatty acids are solid at room temperature, while unsaturated fatty acids are liquid at room temperature.

### Fatty Acids to Avoid

Of these, **saturated fatty acids** and **trans fatty acids** are generally considered unhealthy.

Saturated fatty acids increase the risk of obesity, high cholesterol, and heart disease.

Studies show that excessive long-term consumption of trans fatty acids produced through hydrogenation can disrupt blood lipid metabolism, increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease. Some studies also suggest that it may increase the risk of chronic conditions such as diabetes and obesity.

The World Health Organization recommends keeping dietary trans fatty acids as low as possible to promote cardiovascular health, with a maximum intake of no more than 1% of total energy. In other words, assuming an average adult intake of 2000 kilocalories per day, daily trans fatty acid intake should not exceed 2.2 grams.

GB 28050-2011 requires that when food ingredients contain hydrogenated and/or partially hydrogenated oils or fats, or these are used during production, the amount of trans fat (fatty acids) must also be listed in the nutrition facts panel.

### Trans Fatty Acids in Food

According to relevant surveys, the average trans fatty acid content of baked goods (pastries, cookies, bread, etc.), condiments, and fried foods is between 0.30~0.50 g/100g.

So there is no need to worry too much: the trans fatty acids in everyday foods are not enough to endanger health. Still, as a precaution, you may want to check the `Trans Fat (Fatty Acids)` amount listed in the nutrition facts panel when buying snacks.

### Trans Fatty Acids in Cooking

According to a 2021 survey, the average trans fatty acid content of vegetable oils in China is 0.86 g/100g, so there is no need to worry too much.

One source of trans fatty acids that deserves extra attention is the cooking process:

Vegetable oils often contain a high proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which have relatively poor heat stability and readily turn into trans fats at high temperatures.

We therefore need to choose oils appropriately for different uses and minimize how long they are heated.

### Choosing Vegetable Oils

| Oil | Saturated Fatty Acids (%) | Omega 3 (%) | Omega 6 (%) | Omega 9 (%) |
| :----: | :----: | :----: | :----: | :----: |
| Canola oil | 7% | 11% | 21% | 61% |
| Flaxseed oil | 9% | 57% | 16% | 18% |
| Sunflower oil | 12% | 1% | 71% | 16% |
| Corn oil | 13% | 1% | 57% | 29% |
| Olive oil | 15% | 1% | 9% | 75% |
| Soybean oil | 15% | 8% | 54% | 23% |
| Peanut oil | 19% | 0% | 33% | 48% |
| Cottonseed oil | 27% | 0% | 54% | 19% |
| Lard | 43% | 1% | 9% | 47% |
| Palm oil | 51% | 0% | 10% | 39% |
| Beef tallow | 68% | 1% | 3% | 28% |
| Coconut oil | 91% | 0% | 2% | 7% |

* `Peanut oil` is rich in `monounsaturated fat`. However, only high-quality oil is recommended. When cooking with it, also take care not to heat it for too long, to avoid producing `trans fatty acids`.
* `Olive oil` is rich in `monounsaturated fat`, which has only one unsaturated bond. Olive oil is low in `saturated fatty acids`.
    * **Cold dishes/low-temperature cooking:** Choose **extra virgin olive oil**, which retains more antioxidants such as polyphenols. However, it has a lower smoke point (about 160-190℃) and is unsuitable for high-heat stir-frying.
    * **Medium- to high-temperature stir-frying:** Choose **refined olive oil** (labeled Pure or Refined), which has a higher smoke point (above 230℃) and is better suited to Chinese cooking.
* `Soybean oil` contains about 15% `saturated fatty acids`, as well as linoleic acid and vitamins. However, its high polyunsaturated fatty acid content makes it unstable and prone to producing `trans fatty acids` during cooking. It is therefore not recommended for regular high-heat stir-frying, but can be used for cold dishes or ordinary stewing and simmering.
* `Rapeseed oil` has relatively good heat stability.
    * **Traditional rapeseed oil:** It may contain high levels of erucic acid, and consuming large amounts over the long term may be harmful to cardiovascular health.
    * **Double-low rapeseed oil (canola oil):** It has been bred to have extremely low erucic acid levels (<2%) and an excellent fatty acid profile (high in monounsaturated fatty acids and containing Omega-3). It is currently highly recommended by nutrition experts as an everyday stir-frying oil.
* `Coconut oil` is very high in `saturated fatty acids` and has excellent heat stability, but note that some foods use hydrogenated coconut oil. It is suitable for pan-frying and deep-frying in the kitchen.
* `Palm oil` is very high in `saturated fatty acids`, has good heat stability, and is commonly used in commercial frying. Frequent consumption increases the risk of high cholesterol.
* Animal fats such as `lard` and `beef tallow` are rich in `saturated fatty acids` and have excellent heat stability. Although they are less likely to produce harmful substances at high temperatures, their potential negative effects on cardiovascular health mean that intake should be limited. They are not recommended as your sole cooking fat over the long term.

Based on the table above, we can draw several conclusions:

* No oil is perfect; each has advantages and disadvantages. Choose different oils for different cooking situations (cold dishes, heating, and frying).
* **Avoid relying on just one oil:** People today often consume too much Omega-6 and far too little Omega-3. Keep 2-3 different types of oil at home and alternate among them.
* To avoid consuming too many `trans fatty acids`, do not use oils with poor heat stability for heating (such as soybean or corn oil for high-heat stir-frying), and do not heat oils for too long.
* Do not eat large amounts of pan-fried or deep-fried foods. Although saturated fat is heat-stable, excessive saturated fat intake is itself a burden on health.
* Do not reuse oil. Repeated heating produces large amounts of `trans fatty acids` and oxidation products.
* Do not rely on takeout food over the long term, because it is hard to know what oils are being used (usually inexpensive, unstable fats).

#### Stir-Frying Oils (Medium Heat)

* Canola oil (double-low rapeseed oil)
* Peanut oil
* Refined olive oil

These oils contain more monounsaturated fatty acids and have moderate or high smoke points, making them suitable for most Chinese stir-frying needs.

#### Pan-Frying and Deep-Frying Oils (High Heat)

* Coconut oil
* Palm oil
* Lard/beef tallow

High-heat stir-frying and deep-frying require saturated fats with better heat stability, which produce fewer harmful oxidation products. For cardiovascular health, however, **the most fundamental strategy is to fry less often**.

#### Oils for Cold Dishes, Low-Temperature Cooking, and Stewing

* Flaxseed oil
* Perilla oil
* Extra virgin olive oil
* Sesame oil/walnut oil

Flaxseed oil and perilla oil are rich in the exceptionally valuable **Omega-3**, but they are extremely sensitive to heat: even slight heating causes oxidation, spoilage, and off odors. Using them in cold dishes is a "perfect-score strategy" for getting more Omega-3.
